## What a buyer is actually underwriting

Manufacturing is the sector where a buyer is purchasing the least amount of goodwill and the most amount of verifiable capability. Certifications, customer approvals, equipment and skilled people are all things that take years to build and cannot be assembled quickly. That is why this sector attracts the widest buyer pool on this site, including overseas acquirers who want a Singapore base and cannot get one any other way.

The question a buyer is answering is whether that capability keeps producing after the founder retires. In a sector where the founder is often the person who understands the process best, that is not automatic.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range here is 4x to 6x normalised EBITDA. Normalised means reported profit adjusted for owner-specific items: your salary brought to what it would cost to employ someone to do the job, one-off costs removed, and related-party rent brought to a market rate where the property is held personally.

That last adjustment matters more in this sector than any other. A great many Singapore manufacturers occupy premises the owner holds in their own name, at a rent set for reasons that have nothing to do with the market. Whichever direction it has been set, normalising it changes the earnings figure the multiple is applied to, and a buyer will do that arithmetic whether or not you do.

Within the range, the differentiators are:

- **Customer tenure and spread.** Twenty years of approved supply to a diversified base is close to the top of the range. The same profit from one customer is not.
- **Certification depth.** Transferable ISO and customer-specific approvals, with the change-of-control position already checked.
- **Equipment condition.** Not age alone but condition, maintenance records and a stated replacement plan.
- **Management depth.** A works manager or engineering lead who is not the owner and who intends to stay.

## An illustrative example

Illustrative figures, chosen to show the mechanics.

A precision machining business turns over S$6.5m with reported profit of S$700,000. The owner pays themselves S$120,000, below the S$180,000 an operations lead would cost, so normalisation reduces earnings by S$60,000. The company pays the owner S$240,000 a year in rent for a factory they hold personally, against a market rent of about S$300,000, so a further S$60,000 comes out. Normalised EBITDA is therefore closer to S$580,000 than the S$700,000 reported.

At 4x that is roughly S$2.3m, and at 6x roughly S$3.5m. Two lessons sit in that arithmetic. First, the headline profit was not the number anyone was ever going to pay on. Second, the S$1.2m spread across the range is decided by certifications, customer tenure and management depth, none of which can be fixed in the three months before a sale.

## Succession is the real driver here

A large cohort of Singapore manufacturing owners built their businesses through the 1980s and 1990s and are now past retirement age with no family successor. That is the single most common reason these businesses come to market, and it has a practical consequence worth understanding.

Buyers know it too. An owner who is visibly out of time has less leverage than one who chose the moment. The way to keep that leverage is to start early, while continuing to run the business well, so that the sale is a decision rather than a necessity. Owners who begin the conversation two years out consistently do better than those who begin when they have already stopped enjoying the work.

## What surfaces in diligence

Three things reliably appear in manufacturing diligence and are worth resolving first.

Workplace safety and environmental records are examined closely. An unresolved incident or an outstanding notice does not usually kill a deal, but it delays completion, and delay is where deals lose momentum.

Customer approval clauses, as above. Check them.

Related-party arrangements: property, but also any supply or service arrangement with a company connected to the owner. These need to be either terminated cleanly or documented at arm's length before the buyer's lawyers find them.

## Where to start

Get an indicative range using your normalised figures rather than your reported ones, so the number means something. If the gap to what you need is large, it is almost always customer concentration or management depth, and both take time rather than money.
