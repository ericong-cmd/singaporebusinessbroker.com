## What a buyer is actually underwriting

Automotive covers several quite different businesses, and the first useful step is to be clear which one you are selling. A service workshop, a parts distributor, a dealership and a fleet services provider are underwritten in different ways, even though they share a sector page.

What they have in common is that value tends to concentrate in three places: property, rights, and people. Property because premises with the right zoning and access are scarce. Rights because franchise and distribution agreements are hard to obtain. People because a workshop without its technicians is a building with equipment in it.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range is 3x to 4.5x normalised EBITDA, applied to profit adjusted for owner salary at market, one-off items and identifiable personal expenses, and smoothed across the COE cycle.

Within it:

- **Franchise and distribution rights,** and whether they survive a change of control.
- **Technician retention,** particularly where specific skills drive customer return.
- **Property position,** owned, leased with term, or short-dated.
- **Revenue mix,** with recurring servicing and fleet contracts worth more than one-off retail work.

## An illustrative example

Illustrative figures, chosen to show the mechanics.

A workshop and parts business turns over S$4.4m with profit averaging S$470,000 across three years. The owner takes S$110,000 against S$160,000 for a general manager, so normalisation reduces earnings by S$50,000. Normalised EBITDA is about S$420,000.

At 3x that is roughly S$1.26m, at 4.5x roughly S$1.89m. If the premises are owned and included, they are valued separately and added, which is why headline prices in this sector can look inconsistent from the outside: some include property and some do not.

## Decide the property question first

Of all the choices on this page, this is the one to settle before you speak to anybody.

Including the property produces a larger number but a smaller pool of buyers, since fewer parties can fund both a trading business and real estate. Retaining it and leasing to the buyer keeps the business accessible to trade buyers and operators, but you then need a properly documented lease at market terms with sensible tenure, because no buyer will accept a lease from a connected landlord that could be changed later.

Both routes work. Making the decision late, after buyers are engaged, is what causes problems.

## Recurring servicing is the underrated asset

Owners in this sector tend to lead with turnover, and turnover in automotive is dominated by lumpy, cycle-driven sales.

What a buyer values more highly is the boring recurring part: scheduled servicing, fleet maintenance contracts, corporate accounts that return every quarter. That revenue is predictable, it is not tied to a COE cycle, and it survives a change of ownership because customers return for convenience and record rather than for a relationship. If you have this and have never separated it out in your reporting, doing so before you go to market lets a buyer see the stable core rather than only the volatility around it.
