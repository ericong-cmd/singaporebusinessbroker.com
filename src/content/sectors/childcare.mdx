## What a buyer is actually underwriting

Childcare has an unusual combination for an SME sector: predictable revenue, a regulated barrier to entry, and demand that does not track the economy. Parents enrol a child and, absent a problem, keep them enrolled. That produces earnings a buyer can forecast with real confidence, which is why the multiples here sit at the top of the site.

The risks are correspondingly specific. Licensing, the principal, and the physical premises. Each of them can stop a deal rather than merely reprice it.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range is 5x to 8x normalised EBITDA, applied to profit adjusted for owner salary at market rate, one-off costs and identifiable personal expenses.

Within it:

- **Occupancy against licensed capacity,** and the trend across several intakes.
- **Principal and teacher retention,** with the principal's intentions ideally documented.
- **Licence and premises compliance,** current, with change-of-control implications understood.
- **Number of centres.** Consolidators pay for scale, and multi-centre groups attract competing bidders.
- **Lease tenure,** since premises conditions are tied to the licence.

## An illustrative example

Illustrative figures, chosen to show the mechanics.

A two-centre preschool group turns over S$3.1m with reported profit of S$520,000. The owner draws S$100,000 while also acting as principal at one centre; replacing that role properly costs S$110,000, so normalisation reduces earnings by around S$10,000 net of the salary already taken. Say normalised EBITDA of S$510,000.

At 5x that is roughly S$2.55m, at 8x roughly S$4.08m.

Then the completion adjustments. If S$240,000 of fees have been collected for teaching not yet delivered, plus deposits held, that reduces the proceeds. This is the most common source of disappointment in childcare sales, and it is entirely predictable if the calculation is done early.

## The three things that stop deals here

Unlike most sectors, where a bad finding means a lower price, childcare has findings that end transactions.

An unlicensed or non-compliant branch is the first. A buyer cannot knowingly acquire a regulatory defect in a business that looks after children.

Premises that do not meet the conditions attached to the licence is the second, particularly where a fix requires works or landlord consent.

Any unresolved child safety or welfare matter is the third, and it is not a commercial negotiation.

None of this is a reason for pessimism. It is a reason to have your compliance position genuinely in order, at every branch, before a buyer's advisers look at it.
