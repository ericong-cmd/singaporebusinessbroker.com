## What a buyer is actually underwriting

A trading or distribution business is, from a buyer's point of view, a set of rights and a working capital engine. The rights are your agency and distribution agreements. The engine is the stock and receivables that turn those rights into cash.

Both need examining, and owners tend to focus on the first while the second decides how much money actually reaches them.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range is 3x to 5x normalised EBITDA, applied to profit adjusted for owner salary, one-off costs and identifiable personal expenses.

The factors that move you within it:

- **Agreement quality.** Exclusive, long-dated, with change-of-control handled. This is the single largest determinant.
- **Principal relationships.** Held by the company, with more than one person who knows them.
- **Customer spread.** A distributor selling to two hundred retailers is safer than one selling to three chains.
- **Stock discipline.** Turns, ageing and obsolescence, which tell a buyer whether you buy well.

## An illustrative example

Illustrative figures, chosen to show the mechanics.

A distributor turns over S$9m with reported profit of S$650,000. The owner draws S$150,000, roughly market for the role, so no material salary adjustment. A one-off bad debt write-off of S$70,000 is added back as non-recurring, giving normalised EBITDA of about S$720,000.

At 3x that is roughly S$2.16m, at 5x roughly S$3.6m.

Then the balance sheet arrives. Suppose the business normally runs S$2.1m of working capital, and stock includes S$300,000 of lines that have not moved in eighteen months. The buyer writes that stock down, and expects the business to be delivered with a normal working capital level. Both adjustments come out of the seller's proceeds, not the buyer's pocket. An owner who has cleared slow stock in the year before sale is simply better off.

## The change-of-control clause

If you read one clause in your own agreements before speaking to any buyer, read this one.

Distribution and agency agreements frequently allow the principal to terminate, renegotiate or withhold consent on a change of ownership. A buyer will find it, and the consequence is predictable: either the price falls, or a large part of it is deferred until the agreements have demonstrably survived.

The way to manage this is to know your position first, and where you can, to speak to key principals in confidence early. It feels risky. It is far less risky than a buyer raising it at term sheet stage when you have no time to fix anything.
