## What a buyer is actually underwriting

Construction is priced lower than most sectors on this site, and it is worth being direct about why. It is not that the businesses are worse run. It is that project-based earnings give a buyer less to rely on. Every year the work has to be won again, margins are set at tender against costs that may move, and the balance sheet holds retentions, bonds and work-in-progress that make the true financial position harder to read than in a service business.

What a buyer is looking for, then, is evidence of discipline: that you price work properly, that you deliver it at the margin you tendered, and that the order book in front of them is real.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range is 2.5x to 4x normalised EBITDA. Normalisation here means the usual owner adjustments, plus something specific to this sector: smoothing across enough years to see through the project cycle. A single strong year on one large contract is not a run rate, and presenting it as one damages credibility.

Within the range, four things matter:

- **BCA grading.** What you are entitled to tender for, and whether the grading survives the deal structure.
- **Order book quality.** Contracted and commenced work with verifiable margin, not a list of tenders submitted.
- **Margin consistency.** Tendered margin versus delivered margin, across projects. This is the clearest evidence of competence a contractor can show.
- **Client relationships.** Approved vendor status held by the company rather than by the owner personally.

## An illustrative example

Illustrative figures, chosen to show the mechanics.

An M&E contractor turns over S$12m with reported profit averaging S$800,000 across three years, though the individual years were S$1.3m, S$400,000 and S$700,000. The owner takes S$180,000 against a market rate of S$200,000 for the managing role, so normalisation reduces earnings slightly, to about S$780,000.

At 2.5x that is roughly S$1.95m, at 4x roughly S$3.1m. Note the importance of using the three-year average rather than the S$1.3m year: a buyer would have insisted on it, and an owner who anchors on the peak year spends the whole negotiation being argued down from a number that was never available.

Then the balance sheet items come in. Retentions receivable, bonds outstanding and work-in-progress valuation are each negotiated separately from the multiple, and they can move the final proceeds materially. This is why in construction the completion mechanism matters nearly as much as the price.

## Getting the order book ready to be examined

The most useful preparation in this sector is to be able to hand a buyer a clean schedule of current work: contract value, costs incurred to date, costs to complete, margin at tender, margin now, and the reason for any difference.

Contractors who can produce that document are treated as credible and hold their price. Contractors who cannot invite the buyer to assume the worst about every project they cannot see into. The schedule takes work to build the first time and very little to maintain afterwards.

## Where to start

Work out your three-year normalised average rather than your best year, then get an indicative range. If the number disappoints, the two things that move it are margin consistency and getting client relationships onto the company's name.
