## What a buyer is actually underwriting

Events and media agencies are people businesses selling project work, which is the combination buyers find hardest to underwrite. The revenue does not recur automatically, and the relationships that generate it often belong to individuals rather than the company.

That said, agencies do sell, and the ones that sell well have found a way to make at least part of their revenue predictable and at least part of their client base institutional.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range is 2.5x to 4.5x normalised EBITDA, applied to profit adjusted for owner salary at market and one-off items, and averaged across enough years to smooth project lumpiness.

Within it:

- **Retained versus project revenue.** The single biggest differentiator.
- **Client tenure and spread,** and whether relationships sit with account leads or only with the founder.
- **Owned formats and IP,** properly assigned to the company.
- **Team continuity,** particularly creative and account leadership.

## An illustrative example

Illustrative figures, chosen to show the mechanics.

An agency reports S$900,000 profit in a year that included one large campaign, against S$310,000 and S$380,000 in the two years either side. A buyer will not treat S$900,000 as the run rate. Averaging and excluding the outlier suggests something closer to S$400,000, and after adjusting the founder's S$120,000 salary to a S$170,000 market rate, normalised EBITDA is about S$350,000.

At 2.5x that is roughly S$875,000, at 4.5x roughly S$1.58m.

The instructive part is the S$500,000 of profit that disappeared in normalisation. It was real money in a real year, and it is still not something a buyer will pay a multiple on, because a multiple prices repeatability. Owners who understand this before negotiating do much better than owners who discover it during.

## The IP audit nobody does until it is too late

Agencies accumulate creative work over years, often with freelancers and often under client contracts that were signed quickly.

Two questions matter. Do your client contracts assign IP to the client, and if so, which of your supposedly repeatable formats do you actually own. And did the freelancers who produced the work sign assignments, or does the copyright still sit with them.

Where a buyer is paying for formats they can reuse, these questions decide whether they are buying anything. It is worth resolving now, while the people involved are still contactable.
