## What a buyer is actually underwriting

Logistics is one of the more straightforward SME sectors to sell, for a reason that has nothing to do with margins: the earnings are modelable. A buyer looking at a freight forwarder or a 3PL can see volumes, lanes, contract terms and warehouse commitments, and build a forecast they believe. Compare that with a business whose revenue is a large number of small discretionary purchases, and you can see why the starting multiple sits higher.

What a buyer is testing is durability. Will these volumes still be here in two years, and will they still be here without you. Everything below is a version of that question.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range for logistics and freight on this page is 3.5x to 5.5x normalised EBITDA. Normalised means your reported profit adjusted for owner-specific items: your own salary brought to a market rate for the job, one-off costs removed, personal expenses stripped out where they can be identified line by line.

Within that range, four things do most of the work:

- **Contracted versus spot.** Committed volume on multi-year terms is worth materially more per dollar of profit than the same profit earned on spot work, because one is a forecast and the other is a hope.
- **Concentration.** A book spread across thirty customers with none above fifteen percent prices very differently from the same revenue with one customer at forty.
- **Assets and their condition.** A young, well-maintained fleet with records is neutral to positive. An old fleet with deferred maintenance is a deduction, because the buyer is inheriting capital expenditure they did not plan.
- **Systems.** A real warehouse or transport management system means the operation survives a change of ownership. Spreadsheets plus the owner's memory means it might not.

## An illustrative example

These figures are illustrative, chosen to show the mechanics rather than taken from a transaction.

A freight forwarder turns over S$8m with reported profit of S$620,000. The owner draws S$220,000, above the S$150,000 a general manager would cost, so normalisation adds S$70,000 back. A one-off legal cost of S$40,000 is also added back. Normalised EBITDA is around S$730,000.

At 3.5x that is roughly S$2.55m. At 5.5x it is roughly S$4.0m. The S$1.45m difference is decided almost entirely by the four factors above, and mostly before a buyer is ever contacted.

Now assume one customer represents 45 percent of that revenue. In practice a buyer does not simply move down the range. They are more likely to hold the headline number and put S$800,000 of it into an earn-out contingent on that customer still being there in two years. Whether you receive the full price then depends on something you no longer control.

## Rate cycles and what to show

Freight rates move, sometimes violently, and a valuation based on a single exceptional year will not survive diligence.

The practical answer is to present the normalisation yourself. Show three to five years, explain the cycle, and identify what is structural growth in your business versus what was the market. Owners who do this are treated as credible and tend to hold their number. Owners who lead with their best year invite a buyer to pick a different one.

## Fleet, premises and the capital question

Two items reliably surface late and cost money when they do.

Deferred fleet maintenance is the first. A buyer's technical diligence will establish the real condition of the vehicles, and anything overdue becomes a deduction from the price, usually at more than it would have cost you to do it. Servicing on schedule with records kept is cheaper than the discount.

Warehouse tenure is the second. Remaining term and assignability matter here for the same reason they matter in retail: a buyer purchasing a business whose premises are up for renewal in a year is purchasing a negotiation with a landlord. Where you have security of tenure, evidence it early.

## Where to start

Get an indicative range first. If the number is close to what you need, the work is packaging and process. If it is not, the gap is almost always concentration, contracts or systems, and all three respond to twelve to eighteen months of deliberate effort.
