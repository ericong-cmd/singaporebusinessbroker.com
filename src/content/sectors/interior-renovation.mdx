## What a buyer is actually underwriting

Fit-out and renovation share the fundamental difficulty of all project businesses: this year's revenue tells you very little about next year's. Every job has to be won, priced and delivered, and the profit is made or lost in the execution rather than in the sale.

A buyer is therefore looking for evidence of two things. That you can win work repeatedly without the founder charming every client personally, and that you can deliver at the margin you tendered.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range is 2.5x to 4x normalised EBITDA, applied to profit adjusted for owner salary at market and one-off items, and averaged across enough years to see through project lumpiness.

Within it:

- **Client type.** Repeat commercial and institutional clients versus one-off residential work.
- **Margin discipline.** Tendered versus delivered margin, consistently.
- **Order book.** Signed and commenced work with verifiable margin.
- **Team depth.** A design lead and project managers who are not the owner.
- **Brand versus personal reputation.** Where the enquiries actually come from.

## An illustrative example

Illustrative figures, chosen to show the mechanics.

A commercial fit-out firm averages S$480,000 profit across three years on turnover of S$6m, with individual years of S$720,000, S$210,000 and S$510,000. The owner takes S$160,000, roughly market, so no material salary adjustment. Normalised EBITDA is around S$480,000.

At 2.5x that is roughly S$1.2m, at 4x roughly S$1.92m.

Then working capital. Fit-out businesses fund materials and labour ahead of payment, and a buyer expects the business delivered with a normal level of working capital in it, plus a negotiated position on retentions and work in progress. In a sector where a lot of cash is tied up in projects at any moment, this part of the negotiation frequently matters more to the seller's proceeds than a quarter turn on the multiple.

## Build the project schedule

The most valuable preparation here is the same as in construction: a project-by-project schedule showing contract value, costs to date, costs to complete, tendered margin, current margin, and the reason for any variance.

This document does two things. It lets a buyer verify the order book instead of discounting it, and it demonstrates the estimating discipline that is the core skill in this business. Firms that can produce it are treated differently from firms that cannot.

## Working capital is the quiet negotiation

Fit-out businesses carry an unusual amount of cash inside live projects, and this is where sellers most often lose money they were not expecting to lose.

A buyer will calculate a normal working capital level from your history and expect the business handed over with that much in it. Anything below is deducted. Retentions sitting with clients, sometimes years old, are money you earned but a buyer will not pay full value for. And defect liability periods run past completion, so someone has to carry them. None of these appear in the headline multiple, and together they can matter more to your proceeds than the multiple does.
