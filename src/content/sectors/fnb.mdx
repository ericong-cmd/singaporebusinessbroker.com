## What a buyer is actually underwriting

Two F&B businesses with the same profit rarely sell for the same money. The gap is confidence: how quickly a buyer can verify the earnings, and how much of the operation walks out of the door with you.

Think about what an acquirer is really buying. Not last year's profit, which is already spent, but the likelihood that the same profit arrives next year without you there. Everything that makes that likelihood easier to believe raises the multiple. Everything that makes it a matter of faith lowers it.

In F&B that verification problem is unusually sharp. The revenue is high-frequency and low-value, so it cannot be checked against a handful of contracts. The cost base moves with manpower rules and rent reviews. And a meaningful part of what makes the place work is often standing in the kitchen.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range for F&B on this page is 2.5x to 4x normalised EBITDA. That is a starting point, not a valuation, and the spread between the two ends is wide for a reason: it is where all the sector-specific risk gets expressed.

Normalised EBITDA is your reported profit with the owner-specific items taken out and put back. Your own above-market or below-market salary is adjusted to what it would cost to employ a manager in your place. One-off costs like a renovation write-off come out. Personal expenses running through the business come out, but only the ones you can identify line by line, because a buyer will not credit an add-back they cannot verify.

What then moves you within the range is straightforward to list and slow to fix:

- **Number of outlets.** One outlet is a job. Three or more with consistent unit economics is a business, and it prices like one.
- **Lease security.** Long remaining term with assignment permitted, versus a renewal due next year.
- **Kitchen dependency.** Documented recipes and a central kitchen, versus a head chef whose departure changes the product.
- **Revenue quality.** Repeat dine-in and catering contracts, versus delivery platform volume at thin or negative contribution.
- **Financial clarity.** Outlet-level P&L a buyer's accountant can reconcile, versus group figures with no breakdown.

## An illustrative example

The numbers below are illustrative, chosen to show the mechanics rather than drawn from a transaction.

Take a three-outlet cafe group turning over S$4.2m with reported profit of S$380,000. The owner pays themselves S$60,000, well below the S$140,000 it would cost to hire an operations manager to do the job, so normalisation reduces earnings by S$80,000. A one-off S$45,000 fit-out write-off in the same year is added back. Normalised EBITDA is therefore around S$345,000.

At the bottom of the range that is roughly S$860,000. At the top it is roughly S$1.38m. The S$520,000 gap between those two numbers is not negotiation. It is the difference between a group where the leases have four years to run, the recipes are documented, an operations manager runs the day to day, and each outlet reports its own P&L, and one where the founder is in the kitchen six days a week and the main lease expires in ten months.

That gap is why preparation is worth more than bargaining. Twelve to eighteen months spent on the four drivers listed above will usually move the price further than any amount of argument at term sheet stage.

## The delivery platform question

Platform revenue deserves its own mention because it distorts more F&B valuations than anything else on this page.

Delivery volume looks like growth on the top line. A buyer will look at it differently: they will strip out the commission, add back the packaging and the incremental labour, and ask what the contribution margin actually is per order. Where the answer is thin, the revenue adds risk without adding value, because it inflates the base a buyer is being asked to pay a multiple on while contributing little profit.

If a large share of your orders come through platforms, work out the true contribution before you go to market. If it is healthy, that is a genuine selling point and you should evidence it. If it is not, you are better off knowing that first than being shown it by a buyer's accountant halfway through diligence.

## Timing the sale

The two questions worth answering before anything else are what the business would fetch today, and whether the gap between that and what you want is closeable in the time you have.

Lease cycles usually set the rhythm. Going to market with a renewal already signed on acceptable terms removes a variable a buyer would otherwise price. Going to market with under two years left invites an offer that assumes the worst.
