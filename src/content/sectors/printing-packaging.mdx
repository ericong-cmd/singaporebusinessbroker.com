## What a buyer is actually underwriting

A buyer looking at printing or packaging is doing two valuations at once: one of your business, and one of your market.

That second assessment is what makes this sector different. In packaging the market view is broadly neutral to positive, because physical goods keep needing to be contained. In commercial print it is negative, and no amount of good management changes the fact that the buyer is underwriting a category under structural pressure.

This is not a reason not to sell. It is a reason to be precise about which part of that spectrum you sit on, and to evidence it.

## How the multiple range is applied

The indicative range is 3x to 4.5x normalised EBITDA, applied to profit adjusted for owner salary at market, one-off items and identifiable personal expenses.

Within it:

- **Product mix.** Packaging and specialist converting versus general commercial print.
- **Customer tenure and approval status.** Long-standing, technically qualified relationships.
- **Margin trend.** Whether you have held price or been eroded, over several years.
- **Equipment condition** and whether major capital spend is imminent.
- **Management depth,** meaning a works manager who is not the owner.

## An illustrative example

Illustrative figures, chosen to show the mechanics.

A packaging converter turns over S$5.5m with reported profit of S$560,000. The owner takes S$130,000 against a market rate of S$170,000, so normalisation reduces earnings by S$40,000. Normalised EBITDA is about S$520,000.

At 3x that is roughly S$1.56m, at 4.5x roughly S$2.34m.

Suppose the main press is fourteen years old with no documented replacement plan, and a buyer's technical adviser estimates S$700,000 to replace it within three years. That does not simply move the multiple down. It becomes an explicit negotiation, and the buyer will argue for something close to a price reduction of that amount. A maintained machine with a costed replacement schedule is a known cost; an unmaintained one with no plan is an unknown, and unknowns are discounted harder than numbers.

## Show the margin trend before you are asked

The most persuasive document a print or packaging seller can produce is a multi-year margin analysis by product line.

If your margins have held while volumes shifted, that is evidence of pricing discipline and a defensible customer base, and it argues directly for the top of the range. If margins have eroded, you are better off presenting that with your own explanation than letting a buyer discover the trend and set the narrative.
